    About these texts

    What you are reading is the first edition of each novel, published between 1811 and 1818, laid out on its own printed pages — carrying the corrections that R. W. Chapman made for his Oxford Clarendon edition of 1923.

    Those are two different books, and the reason for taking one thing from each is that each is better at one job. The first editions are what Austen’s own readers held, and they are the authority for how she spelled: chuse, shew, connexion, expence, surprize, her’s. Later editions quietly tidied all of that away. But the first editions were set by hand, at speed, and the compositors made mistakes — persn for person, purdose for purpose, againt for against, nnight for night, quadriell for quadrille. Chapman collated the early editions and repaired those, and it is his reading text that has been the scholarly standard for most of a century.

    So the two were aligned word by word and the difference kept. It is small: forty-six words in the hundred and twenty-three thousand of Pride and Prejudice, about four hundredths of one per cent. Nearly all of it is Chapman mending the compositor; a little of it is his normalising a spelling (stile to style, stedfastly to steadfastly) or settling a name (Phillips to Philips). A handful are genuine choices between readings — county or country, mite or time. Because the difference is a list of forty-six things rather than a whole rival text, it is kept as a layer over the first edition instead of as a second edition, and it can be switched off.

    Why the pages are the first edition’s. A page number is only useful if it points at something real. Austen’s novels were published as three or four small duodecimo volumes, each restarting its numbering at one, so a citation here names a volume as well as a page — vol. II, p. 143. Those pages are small, about a hundred and forty words, which is why a quotation of any length often spans two of them and is cited as a range.

    Why lines break where they do. Setting by hand meant breaking words across the foot of a page: the first edition of Pride and Prejudice ends one page with Mi and opens the next with chaelmas. The word has been rejoined here, so that searching for Michaelmas finds it, while the page break is remembered in the right place so the citation still names the leaf the word begins on. Running heads, printers’ signatures, small capitals and the opening drop capitals are set as the books set them.

    Who corrected what. Two hands have been over the misprints and the page keeps them apart. The Corrected setting applies the 209 slips Wikisource’s transcribers marked [sic] against the scans, with the reading they judged the compositor meant. Modern corrections lays Chapman’s 1923 editorial readings over the top — 33 more, which go further than repairing a slip: stile becomes style, the British –our spellings are restored, and a gulf the first edition calls impossible he reads as impassable. Where the two agree they agree exactly: of the twenty forms both flag, they choose the same reading twenty times out of twenty. A word only one reading prints can still be searched, and the page says which reading has it and on which leaf.
